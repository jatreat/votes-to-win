How to calculate your win number

Before you knock a door, print a sign or ask anyone for money, you need one number: how many votes it takes to win. Everything else in the campaign works back from it.

What a win number is

Your win number is the number of votes you need to win, plus a cushion for the things you can't control. Campaign managers set it on day one, because it's the number that sizes everything else: how many voters you have to talk to, how many doors that takes, how much mail you can afford, and how much money you have to raise.

Candidates who skip this step tend to campaign by feel. They knock the doors that are convenient, put signs where friends live, and find out on election night whether it was enough. A win number turns that into a target you can check your progress against every week.

The formula

  1. Estimate the ballots that will be cast. Registered voters in your district multiplied by the turnout you expect.
  2. Work out the share you need. In a two-candidate race, half the ballots plus one. With three or more candidates the top vote-getter wins, so plan for roughly 40% in a three-way race, 35% with four, and 30% with five or more. If your state requires a majority or holds a runoff, plan for half plus one no matter how many are running.
  3. Add a cushion. Five percent is a common choice. It covers turnout running higher than you guessed and voters who skip your race on the ballot.

A worked example

Say you're running for a town council seat against one opponent. Your town has 9,000 registered voters and the election is in an off year, when local turnout runs around 22%.

If a third candidate enters, the math changes. You'd plan for 40% of 1,980 ballots, or 792 votes, which becomes 832 with the cushion. Fewer votes, but a less predictable race.

Where the numbers come from

Registered voters. Your county clerk, town clerk or secretary of state publishes registration counts by district, usually as a PDF or spreadsheet. For a ward or precinct race, ask the clerk directly; most will email you the count the same day.

Turnout. The presets above are rough national figures. Your district's own turnout in its last election of the same type is better. If your last off-year municipal election drew 19%, use 19%. Pull the number from the same source that posts results.

Past results. If your race has a recent result to compare against, there's a second way to get a win number: take the last winner's vote total, adjust it for any change you expect in turnout, and add your cushion. This method is better for races where voters pick more than one candidate, like a vote-for-three school board election, because turnout math doesn't tell you how many votes the third-place winner needed.

Three mistakes to avoid

  • Using presidential-year turnout for an off-year race. Turnout in a local election can be a third of what it is in November of a presidential year. Match the election type.
  • Counting population instead of registered voters. A town of 12,000 people might have 8,500 registered voters and 1,900 who actually show up for a council race. Only the last number pays your win number.
  • Skipping the cushion. Winning by one vote is a recount. A five percent cushion is the cheapest insurance you'll buy all campaign.

Questions

What is a win number in a political campaign?

The number of votes a candidate needs to win the race, plus a cushion. It is calculated from the ballots expected (registered voters times turnout) and the share needed to win, which is half plus one in a two-candidate race.

How do you calculate the votes needed to win an election?

Multiply registered voters by expected turnout to estimate ballots cast, then take half plus one for a two-way race (or about 40% for three candidates, 35% for four, 30% for five or more), and add a cushion of around 5%.

What turnout should I use for a local election?

Use your district's turnout in its last election of the same type. If you can't find it, rough national figures are about 66% in a presidential year, 50% in a midterm, 22% for off-year and local elections, and 20% for primaries.
