Campaign plan template

What goes in a campaign plan for a local race

A campaign plan for a local race isn't a binder. It's six numbers, a calendar and a budget, written down so you can check yourself against them every week.

The guide

Free

Six parts

  1. Win number. The votes you need, plus a cushion. Everything else is built to produce it. How to calculate it.
  2. Voter contact plan. How many of those votes come from talking to voters, how many conversations that takes, and how many doors and calls produce them. The math.
  3. Volunteer plan. The door and phone hours divided into shifts, and who's filling them. Names, not "volunteers."
  4. Budget. Mail, signs, literature, digital, website, filing fee, contingency, and the cost per vote. Line by line.
  5. Fundraising plan. The budget as a goal, the number of asks it takes, the call-time hours, and when the money has to be in the bank. The math.
  6. Calendar. A week-by-week schedule from now to Election Day with door goals, mail drops, early voting and get-out-the-vote.

A 12-week timeline

Weeks 12 to 9: build. Finalize the win number and the plan. Order palm cards and signs (printers need two weeks). Set up the website and a way to give. Start call time: the early money buys everything below. Start knocking doors with a short script and the palm card.

Weeks 8 to 5: persuade. Doors every week at the goal pace. Signs go up as soon as your town allows. Call time continues until the budget is funded. Send the mail house your art and your list for the first piece.

Weeks 4 to 3: mail and finish persuasion. First mail piece drops. Finish the money: the last mail piece has to be paid for three weeks out. Wrap up persuasion doors before early voting opens; after that, every door is a get-out-the-vote door.

Weeks 2 to 1: get out the vote. Second and third mail pieces. Digital ads at full spend. Every supporter you identified gets a knock, a call or a text reminding them when and where to vote. Early voting: ask supporters to vote early so Election Day is about the rest.

Election Day. Volunteers at the polls where allowed, calls to supporters who haven't voted by noon, and the candidate thanking people until the polls close.

How to use it

A plan only works if you look at it. Pick one night a week, count doors knocked, conversations, dollars raised and hours of call time against the goals, and write down the gap. A campaign that's 20% behind on doors in week eight can add shifts. The same campaign in week two can't.

Keep the assumptions written down next to the numbers. If your doors are answering at 35% instead of 25%, you need fewer knocks; if your support rate is 40% instead of 50%, you need more conversations. Adjust the plan, not the goal.

Questions

What should a campaign plan for a local election include?

A win number, a voter contact plan (conversations, doors and calls), a volunteer plan with named shifts, a line-item budget, a fundraising plan with call-time hours and a deadline, and a week-by-week calendar to Election Day.

How far in advance should a local campaign start?

Twelve weeks of door-knocking is a good plan and eight is the minimum. Fundraising should start earlier, because the money has to be in the bank about three weeks before Election Day to pay for the last mail piece.

When should a local campaign send mail?

In the last three to four weeks. A typical program is three pieces: an introduction, one on the main issue, and a get-out-the-vote reminder. Mail houses need art and the list two to three weeks before each drop.
