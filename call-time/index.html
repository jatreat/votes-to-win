How much call time does your campaign need?

Nobody likes call time. Every candidate who wins does it anyway. Here's how to figure out exactly how many hours you need, so you can schedule them and get them over with.

What call time is

Call time is the candidate, on the phone, asking people they know for a specific amount of money. Not an email blast, not a donate button on the website, not a fundraiser with a cash bar. A direct ask from the candidate is the only method that reliably raises money for a first-time local campaign, because the people who give early give because you asked them.

The budget sets the goal. The budget guide works out $3,979 for a 9,000-voter town race. That's the number to raise, less whatever you're putting in yourself.

Four numbers that turn a budget into hours

  • Average gift. For a local race, $75 is a fair planning number. Some people give $25, some give $250, and your average lands in between.
  • Yes rate. About 30% of the people you ask will give something. The rest say no, say later, or don't pick up.
  • People reached per hour. About six. Most calls go to voicemail; the ones that connect take a few minutes.
  • Your own money. Whatever you put in reduces the goal dollar for dollar.

The math

People to ask54 ÷ 30% yes rate180
Hours on the phone180 ÷ 6 people reached an hour30
Call time30 hours

Thirty hours of call time for a $4,000 race. That's the whole fundraising program for a town council seat, and it's less than most candidates spend on social media.

Scheduling it

Fundraising has a deadline that isn't Election Day. The money for your last mail piece has to be in the bank about three weeks out, because the mail house needs to be paid before it prints. Work back from that date.

If you have six months, 30 hours is a little over an hour a week. If you're starting eight weeks out, it's about four hours a week: two two-hour sessions. Put them on the calendar like a doctor's appointment, with someone sitting next to you dialing the next number while you talk. A call-time manager, even a volunteer, roughly doubles what a candidate raises per hour.

Who to call

Start with the list you already have: family, friends, coworkers, neighbors, people from church or the kids' school, anyone who has ever said "let me know if you run." Rank them by how much you think they'd give, and call the top of the list first. Early money matters more than late money because it buys signs and literature while the race is still open.

Ask for a specific number. "Can I count on you for $100?" raises more than "anything you can do helps," and it's easier on the donor, who now knows what you want.

After the call

Every yes gets a thank-you note and a link to give. Every maybe gets a follow-up call in a week. Every no gets thanked and asked to put a sign in the yard. Keep the list in a spreadsheet with a column for the amount pledged and a column for the amount received; the gap between the two is where campaigns lose money.

Questions

How much money does a local campaign need to raise?

Build the budget first. A town race with about 2,000 expected ballots typically needs $3,000 to $5,000, mostly for mail. The raise is that budget less any money the candidate puts in.

How many hours of call time does a candidate need?

Divide the goal by the average gift to get donations needed, divide by the yes rate to get asks, then divide by the people reached per hour. A $4,000 goal at a $75 average gift, 30% yes rate and six people an hour is about 30 hours.

When should campaign fundraising be finished?

About three weeks before Election Day, because the last mail piece has to be paid for before it prints. Schedule call time to finish by then.
