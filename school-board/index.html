Running for school board

How many votes does it take to win a school board seat?

School board elections are low-turnout, down-ballot and often multi-seat, which makes the usual "half plus one" math wrong. Here's how to get the right number.

Why school board math is different

Two things set school board races apart. First, many are at-large and multi-seat: voters pick, say, three candidates from a field of five, and the top three win. Second, they're usually at the bottom of the ballot, so a share of the people who show up skip the race entirely.

In a vote-for-three race there's no "half plus one." Each voter casts up to three votes, so the vote totals don't add up to the number of ballots. What matters is the number of votes it took to finish third last time, because that's the seat you're competing for.

Use the last election, not turnout math

Pull the results of the last school board election from your clerk or school district. Find the vote total of the last winner: the third-place finisher in a three-seat race. That's your starting point. Adjust it for any change you expect in turnout (a hot issue on the ballot or a presidential year pushes it up; a quiet off year pulls it down), then add a cushion.

What 2,041 votes takes

School board races have a built-in advantage: a lot of voters are undecided on two of their three choices. A candidate who shows up at the door and asks for one of those votes gets it more often than a council candidate asking for the only vote.

Using the standard assumptions, 2,041 votes means roughly 5,700 door knocks and 380 door hours over the campaign. That's more than one person can do. Plan on a team: the candidate, a few committed volunteers, and a phone program for the rest. Mail does more of the work in a district this size, so the budget runs higher too; the budget guide shows how to price it.

If the race is single-seat

Some districts elect one member per zone or ward. Then the math is the same as any other race: ballots expected, half plus one, plus a cushion. The win number guide walks through it.

Where the numbers come from

School board results are sometimes posted by the school district rather than the county, and sometimes only in meeting minutes. Ask the district clerk for the certified results of the last two elections. Registration counts for the district come from the county clerk; a school district boundary rarely matches a town boundary, so ask for the number by school district, not by town.

One more thing about the bottom of the ballot

If your school board race shares a ballot with a council or mayoral race, some voters will fill in the top and skip the bottom. Your get-out-the-vote reminders should say which race you're in and where on the ballot it is. The cushion in your win number is for the voters who show up and forget.

Questions

How many votes do you need to win a school board election?

In a multi-seat race, as many as the last winning seat took last time, adjusted for turnout, plus a cushion. If the third-place winner in a three-seat race had 1,850 votes and turnout is expected to rise 5%, the win number is about 2,041.

How do you calculate a win number in a vote-for-three election?

Don't use half plus one. Take the vote total of the last winning seat in the most recent election, adjust it for the change in turnout you expect, and add a cushion of about 5%.

Are school board elections low turnout?

Usually. Many are held in off years or in spring, and the race sits at the bottom of the ballot, so some voters who show up skip it. Plan from the district's own past results rather than national turnout figures.
