How many doors do you need to knock?

Doors win local races, but "knock as many as you can" isn't a plan. Here's how to turn your win number into a door goal, a volunteer-hour goal and a weekly shift schedule.

Start from your win number, not from the map

Most first-time candidates pick a door goal by looking at a map and guessing. The better way is to work backward from the votes you need. If you haven't got a win number yet, calculate it first. The example below uses a town of 9,000 registered voters in an off-year election, where the win number came out to 1,041.

Four assumptions that do the work

  • Half your votes come from talking to voters. The other half comes from your name on the ballot, your party or slate, mail, signs and word of mouth. Call it 50%.
  • Half the voters you talk to will back you. That's about right for a candidate who's prepared and listens. It can run higher in a friendly neighborhood and lower in a hostile one.
  • About one door in four answers. On a weekday evening or a Saturday, 25% is a fair planning number. Leave literature at the rest.
  • A volunteer knocks about 15 doors an hour. Suburban streets run faster, rural roads slower.

Most campaigns also do some of their voter contact by phone. In the example, 70% of conversations happen at the door and 30% on the phone, where roughly one call in ten reaches a voter and a volunteer dials about 30 numbers an hour.

That looks like a lot, and it is. It is also entirely doable with a candidate and a handful of volunteers over 12 weeks. The trick is dividing it into shifts.

Turning it into a schedule

Spread 195 door hours over 12 weeks and you need about 16 hours a week at the door, which is six three-hour shifts. Add 105 phone hours and you need another three three-hour phone shifts a week. A candidate who knocks four shifts a week, plus two volunteers covering a shift each and a couple of friends on the phones, hits the number.

Two rules make this work:

  • Front-load it. The last two weeks belong to get-out-the-vote, not persuasion. Plan to finish your persuasion doors before early voting opens, then switch to reminding supporters to vote.
  • Track it weekly. Count doors knocked and conversations had every week against the goal. If you're behind in week four, you can fix it. In week ten you can't.

Which doors

Knock the doors of people who actually vote in elections like yours. Your voter file, which your county or state party or a vendor can provide, shows each voter's history. A household that voted in the last two municipal elections is worth three that only vote for president. If you can't get a voter file, your clerk's list of who voted last time is the next best thing.

Skip the strongest supporters of your opponent. You're looking for persuadable voters and your own supporters who need a reminder.

Literature is part of the door count

Three out of four doors won't answer, so you'll need a palm card or door hanger for all of them, plus a few hundred spares. For the example above that's about 3,300 pieces. Budget for it; the budget guide shows what it costs.

Questions

How many doors should a candidate knock in a local election?

Work back from the win number. For a town of 9,000 registered voters with a win number of 1,041, earning half the votes by voter contact at a 50% support rate and a 25% answer rate means about 2,900 door knocks, or roughly 195 volunteer hours.

How many doors can a volunteer knock in an hour?

About 15 on an average suburban street. Dense neighborhoods run faster and rural roads slower. Plan on three-hour shifts, which is about 45 doors per volunteer per shift.

What percentage of doors answer when canvassing?

Plan on about one in four. Weekday evenings and Saturdays are the best times. Leave a palm card or door hanger at the doors that don't answer.
