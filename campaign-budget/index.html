How much does a local campaign cost?

A local campaign doesn't need a lot of money, but it needs the right amount in the right places, on time. Here's how to build the budget from your win number instead of guessing.

Size the budget from the race, not from your savings

The question isn't how much you can spend. It's what it costs to reach the voters who will decide your race, and whether you can raise that. Start with the ballots you expect and your win number, and let the budget fall out of the voter-contact plan.

The example below is the same 9,000-voter town used in the other guides: about 1,980 ballots expected, a win number of 1,041, and roughly 2,900 doors to knock.

What goes in a local campaign budget

Mail. The biggest line in most local races. Mail goes to likely-voter households, not to everyone, and there are about 1.5 voters per household. Three pieces, timed over the last few weeks, is a typical program: one introduction, one on your main issue, one get-out-the-vote reminder. A printed and mailed postcard runs roughly 50 to 70 cents a piece at local-race volumes.

Yard signs. Signs don't persuade many voters, but they tell your supporters they aren't alone and make you look like a real campaign. You need fewer than you think: about four signs for every hundred ballots expected, placed on busy roads and at supporters' homes, beats hundreds scattered on medians. An 18 by 24 inch sign with an H-stake costs around $4 to $6 in bulk.

Door literature. A palm card or door hanger for every door you knock, since three in four won't answer, plus 10% spare. A simple card runs about 8 cents each at a few thousand pieces.

Digital. A few hundred dollars of targeted social ads in your district, mostly in the last three weeks, is enough for a town race. More than that buys diminishing returns until you have mail and doors covered.

Website and email. One page that says who you are, why you're running and how to help, plus an email tool. A few hundred dollars covers it.

Filing fee. Many towns charge nothing; some charge a filing fee. Check with your clerk.

Contingency. Ten percent for the sign order you didn't plan, the printer's rush fee and the extra mail piece you'll want in the last week.

That's about $3.82 per vote in your win number. For a town race that's typical; it climbs in bigger districts where mail does more of the work, and it falls when the candidate and volunteers knock enough doors to drop a mail piece.

What you can cut, and what you can't

If money is tight, cut in this order: digital first, then one mail piece, then signs. Don't cut door literature; it's the cheapest thing on the list and it's what the voter holds in their hand after you leave. Don't cut the get-out-the-vote mail piece; it's the one that turns supporters into votes.

What you can't cut is timing. Mail houses need your art and your list two to three weeks before the drop date. Sign printers need two weeks. A budget that's fully funded a week before Election Day is a budget that didn't get spent.

Where the money comes from

A $4,000 budget is 54 donations of $75. At a 30% yes rate that's 180 asks, which is about 30 hours of call time. The call-time guide shows how to schedule it, and the calculator builds the whole ladder for your numbers.

Questions

How much does it cost to run for city council or school board?

A town-sized race with about 2,000 expected ballots typically costs $3,000 to $5,000, most of it mail. The example above comes to $3,979, or about $3.82 per vote needed. Larger districts cost more because mail replaces doors.

What is the biggest expense in a local campaign?

Mail. Three postcards to likely-voter households is usually more than half the budget. Yard signs, door literature and digital ads are smaller lines.

How many yard signs do I need for a local election?

About four for every hundred ballots expected, placed on busy roads and at supporters' homes. For a race with 2,000 expected ballots that's around 75 to 100 signs.
